I started with a question about Andrei Tarkovsky's *The Sacrifice*. Then I wondered what became of the son to whom the film was dedicated. That question led backwards—to Tarkovsky's father, the poet Arseny; to revolution and civil war; to his mother's memories; and forwards again, to the directors around the world who have inherited, contested, and transformed his cinema.

The connecting idea is **inheritance**. What can one generation leave another when homes burn, nations change, families separate, and even memory is unreliable?

This is a journey through films and history, not a claim to have decoded the director's symbols. Where an artistic influence is documented, I say so; where I am making a comparison, I treat it as a comparison.

## Table of contents

## The tree: *The Sacrifice* (1986)

A father and his young son plant a tree. The father tells a story about a monk who watered a dead tree every day until it revived. This seemingly futile action will return at the end of Tarkovsky's final film.

The father, Alexander, is a former actor and intellectual living with his family on a Swedish island. During his birthday celebration, news arrives of what seems to be a nuclear catastrophe. In fear, Alexander makes a promise to God: if the disaster is averted, he will give up everything—home, family, possessions, speech. The enigmatic postman Otto suggests that Alexander visit Maria, a woman said to have extraordinary powers. The next morning the world appears normal again. Alexander burns his house to fulfill his vow.

It is tempting to demand an answer: did prayer work? Did Maria save the world? Was the catastrophe a vision, or was Alexander experiencing a psychological crisis? Tarkovsky refuses a definitive explanation. The uncertainty is the film's moral terrain.

**Can a sacrifice be noble when it destroys what other people also love?** Alexander's act may be faith, madness, or both. His family bears its consequences. This is more disturbing than a simple parable about belief.

At the end the child tends the tree and asks about the opening of John's Gospel: "In the beginning was the Word." The father has fallen silent; the child speaks. And the film is dedicated to Tarkovsky's son Andrei, with hope and confidence.

## The son: a reunion in the shadow of death

Andrei Andreevich Tarkovsky was born in 1970. During his father's work abroad in the early 1980s, he remained in the USSR. The Soviet authorities refused permission for him to join his parents in the West, and a dispute over artistic freedom became a separation between father and son.

On **19 January 1986**, fifteen-year-old Andrei and his grandmother finally arrived in Paris. The French president François Mitterrand had appealed to Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev. Contemporary news reports describe the sudden granting of exit visas after years of requests [1].

Tarkovsky was already seriously ill. He died in Paris on **29 December 1986**, aged fifty-four. Father and son had less than a year together after their long separation.

The younger Andrei went on to work in documentary filmmaking and the preservation of his father's legacy. His *Andrey Tarkovsky: A Cinema Prayer* (2019) offers a valuable counterpoint to any retelling of these events: archival material and the father's own words, not just our interpretations.

I cannot help seeing the image of the boy watering the tree as a figure for continuity after a father's death. But this is an interpretation of the film—not a claim to know the son's private motivations.

## The father: Arseny Tarkovsky and the revolution

The filmmaker's father, **Arseny Alexandrovich Tarkovsky (1907–1989)**, was a poet and translator whose verse later entered *Mirror*, *Stalker*, and *Nostalghia*. His mother, **Maria Ivanovna Vishnyakova (1907–1979)**, worked in publishing and literary editing and largely raised Andrei and his sister Marina after the parents separated.

Arseny's life reaches into the world before the director was born. He grew up in Elisavetgrad (today Kropyvnytskyi, Ukraine) during the last years of the Russian Empire. His father, Alexander Karlovich Tarkovsky, had participated in the anti-tsarist revolutionary movement and endured imprisonment and exile.

That history complicates any simplistic portrait of a family eternally opposed to government. The grandfather was himself a revolutionary under the old regime. But revolution did not spare the family from grief.

Arseny's older brother Valery became involved in revolutionary politics and died in 1919 amid fighting against forces associated with Nykyfor Hryhoriv in Ukraine [2]. Arseny was a child when this happened.

A later biographical account describes an extraordinary episode in **1921**: Arseny and friends published an anti-Lenin acrostic, were arrested, and he escaped while being transported for trial [2]. The incident is widely retold, but not every dramatic detail can be checked against accessible contemporary documents; it should be read with that qualification.

Arseny became a poet and translator in Soviet Russia, served as a war correspondent during World War II, was severely wounded, and lost a leg. Remarkably, his first collection of original poems was published only in 1962, the year his son released his first feature film, *Ivan's Childhood*. The British Film Institute places the father's poetic imagination at the heart of the son's cinema [3].

For the maternal family's precise experience in 1917–1921, the widely available biographical record is much thinner. I would rather leave that uncertainty visible than construct an unsupported narrative of confiscations or persecution.

## *Mirror*: the family becomes cinematic time

*Mirror* (1975) is not a conventional autobiography in chronological sequence. It moves like recollection: a mother seated on a fence, wind across a field, a house, wartime sounds, dreams, newsreels, an adult's guilt. The same actress plays figures across generations. And Arseny reads his poems over the images.

Here the father's voice literally inhabits the son's vision. Yet the film is not a private family album. It allows the upheavals of the twentieth century to surface in the intimate textures of childhood.

How do we remember history? Not only through dates, battles, decrees, and names. Sometimes through the tone of a parent's voice, the light in a room, or the fear that someone will not return. *Mirror* reproduces that experience without reducing it to an explanation.

The important technical innovation is duration. Tarkovsky often allows a shot to continue after a plot-driven film would cut. Wind, water, silence and faces have time to become events in their own right. Nature is not merely a code where rain means grief and fire means purification. It remains concrete while gathering emotional force.

This is why knowing the family history enriches *Mirror*—but does not exhaust it.

## *Nostalghia*: leaving without planning to be an exile

Tarkovsky did not originally depart the Soviet Union as a permanent émigré. In 1982 he went to Italy with official permission to make *Nostalghia* (1983), working with Italian collaborators including Tonino Guerra.

He had experienced years of conflict with the Soviet film establishment. *Andrei Rublev* had encountered prolonged release problems; other films and projects faced resistance. International recognition did not eliminate domestic restrictions.

On **10 July 1984**, at a press conference in Milan, he announced his decision not to return to the USSR. Contemporary reporting quotes his complaint that his filmmaking had been repeatedly obstructed and records the unresolved separation from his son [4].

Calling this simply a political defection misses its personal and artistic dimensions. He wanted to make films and to be with his family. Instead, obtaining freedom to work abroad meant losing an ordinary home life in Russia.

*Nostalghia*, completed before the public break, now feels uncannily autobiographical: a Russian intellectual travels in Italy, neither at home abroad nor able to inhabit the homeland he imagines. In the final image, a Russian house seems contained within an Italian abbey. The two landscapes coexist without becoming one.

By *The Sacrifice*, made in Sweden with cinematographer Sven Nykvist, the lost house is no longer only remembered. It is burned.

## Why is Tarkovsky so highly regarded?

It is not because his films are difficult, or because their characters speak philosophically, or because mist and water look beautiful. His achievement is more specific: he expanded cinema's ability to render **experienced time**.

His book *Sculpting in Time* explains why the medium's distinctive material is not simply the moving image, but time impressed into it.

Consider three films:

In ***Solaris* (1972)**, an encounter with an alien intelligence becomes an inquiry into grief, guilt, love, and the limits of knowing another person. The central puzzle cannot be solved with better equipment.

In ***Stalker* (1979)**, a room supposedly fulfills one's deepest desire. But what if we do not know our own deepest desire? The science-fiction premise becomes a test of self-knowledge.

In ***Mirror***, memory itself becomes the structure, rather than information inserted into a conventional story.

The painterly references—to Leonardo, Bruegel and other European masters—the father's poems, Orthodox spiritual imagery and unusual soundscapes all contribute. But imitating the wet floors and long tracking shots misses the point. **A Tarkovskian shot must do something with our sense of time.**

There are legitimate objections: his films can feel excessively slow, his characters can declaim rather than converse, and women are at times treated mainly through men's spiritual quests. Appreciation need not be worship. His achievements are worth serious criticism, not protective reverence.

## The international afterlife: influence and disagreement

Nick James's substantial BFI essay, ["The Tarkovsky legacy"](https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/features/tarkovsky-legacy), is an excellent map of both explicit influence and more subtle aesthetic kinships [3].

**Ingmar Bergman** is a crucial predecessor and interlocutor. *Winter Light* (1963) addresses faith and God's silence; *The Sacrifice* was made with important Swedish collaborators, including Bergman's cinematographer Sven Nykvist. **Robert Bresson's** *Diary of a Country Priest* (1951) and **Michelangelo Antonioni's** *L'Avventura* (1960) provide other routes into spiritual austerity and landscapes of absence.

**Lars von Trier** offers a documented line of influence. He has spoken repeatedly of his admiration for *Mirror* and dedicated *Antichrist* (2009) to Tarkovsky. His *Melancholia* (2011) makes a provocative companion to *The Sacrifice*: the world may end, but the possibility of redemption is entirely different.

**Béla Tarr**, especially with *Werckmeister Harmonies* (2000), teaches us to distrust superficial resemblance. His long takes and apocalyptic atmospheres invite comparisons with Tarkovsky, but Tarr has explicitly contrasted his own material worldview with Tarkovsky's religious hope [3, 5]. Similar form, different metaphysics.

**Nuri Bilge Ceylan** directly acknowledges his debt to Tarkovsky. In *Distant* (*Uzak*, 2002), the sight of someone watching *Stalker* even becomes a joke about aesthetic pretension. *Once Upon a Time in Anatolia* (2011) transforms patient observation into a moral mystery of ordinary people [3, 6].

**Andrey Zvyagintsev's** *The Return* (2003) and *The Banishment* (2007) converse with Tarkovsky in their father figures, water, landscape and moral uncertainty. They should not be mistaken for mere copies.

Other films make illuminating **comparisons rather than demonstrable direct lines of influence**. **Terrence Malick's** *The Tree of Life* (2011) expands a remembered childhood into cosmic history. **Krzysztof Kieślowski's** *Three Colours: Red* (1994) asks about invisible moral connections. **Apichatpong Weerasethakul's** *Uncle Boonmee Who Can Recall His Past Lives* (2010) and *Memoria* (2021) explore forms of memory not confined to one individual.

These differences are the measure of Tarkovsky's reach. Influence matters most when it generates new questions, not perfect reproductions of an established style.

## An eight-film viewing route

I would follow this sequence to study the lineage rather than merely assemble a list of slow films:

1. **Chris Marker, *One Day in the Life of Andrei Arsenevich* (1999).** The filmmaker, his work, and his last years.
2. **Ingmar Bergman, *Winter Light* (1963).** The silence of God before *The Sacrifice*.
3. **Andrei Tarkovsky, *Mirror* (1975).** How an individual remembers a century.
4. **Terrence Malick, *The Tree of Life* (2011).** The family and the universe in one visual thought.
5. **Lars von Trier, *Melancholia* (2011).** An apocalypse without Alexander's bargain.
6. **Béla Tarr and Ágnes Hranitzky, *Werckmeister Harmonies* (2000).** Time without the same promise of transcendence.
7. **Nuri Bilge Ceylan, *Once Upon a Time in Anatolia* (2011).** Mystery and responsibility without supernatural rules.
8. **Apichatpong Weerasethakul, *Memoria* (2021).** Memory at the boundary of consciousness and the world.

Three worthwhile detours are Bresson's *Diary of a Country Priest*, Antonioni's *L'Avventura*, and Zvyagintsev's *The Return*. Then watch *The Sacrifice* again.

## One last tree

Across four generations, one sees repeated disruptions: imperial repression, revolution, civil war, a poet's war wound, the divided household of *Mirror*, bureaucratic restrictions, exile, and a son reunited with his father only months before his death.

It would be too easy to turn this into a myth of a family fated to oppose the state. Their experiences and commitments were more complicated. Nor does biography alone explain why Tarkovsky matters artistically.

What stays with me is a different possibility: **continuity is an action, not a guarantee**.

One filmmaker believes in an act of faith. Another finds only rain and mud. Another hears an inexplicable sound that seems older than herself. Each is responding, in a different language, to questions of time, loss, memory and the limits of knowledge.

At the end of *The Sacrifice*, the child waters a tree that may never revive. He does not know the outcome. Neither do we.

And perhaps this is where an account of Tarkovsky's world influence should end: not with a canon or a ranking, but with somebody continuing the work.
